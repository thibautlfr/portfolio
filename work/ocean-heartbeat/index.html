Ocean Heartbeat

An underwater 3D scene where a heart, a brain and kidneys stand for the ocean's own systems, built overnight for the Nuit de l'Info 2024.

Decisions

  1. The seabed is built from free low-poly models, 716 KB for the eleven the scene loads, since a night left no time to model.
  2. The three organs are mounted once and swapped by scale with GSAP, so a switch never waits on the network.
  3. Caustics are a looping video cast by a spotlight, instead of a water shader the night had no room for.
  4. Retro mode swaps the post-processing chain for one Pixelation pass and leaves the scene untouched.

Context

The Nuit de l’Info is a French student hackathon held overnight. Its 2024 national challenge asked for an educational web app showing how each part of the human body finds an echo in the ocean. Colin Demouge and I entered as a team of two, on the national challenge and five side challenges set by partners, one of which asked for a retro gaming element.

We answered with a scene rather than a page. An organ turns slowly on the seabed, and two panels draw its parallel with the ocean: the heart with the currents that carry heat around the planet, the brain with the ocean as a store of climate data, the kidneys with its filtering.

Constraints

  • One night. The first scene commit landed at 21:28 on 5 December, the handover at 7:40 the next morning. Whatever could not be finished by then had to be dropped early.
  • Two people in one repository. We worked on separate branches and merged into main through the night, so the code had to split cleanly: one component per model, one store for the state both halves read.
  • A jury on any laptop. The scene is judged in a browser on hardware we would never see, so weight and frame cost came before detail.
  • The challenges’ own rules. The national one wanted a deployed URL and the source; the retro one wanted the element reachable from the page, with instructions.

Approach

We split the work by layer. Colin set up the scene, the sand, the fog, the post-processing, the loader, the sound and the interface over the canvas. I took what sits in the scene: the models, their layout on the seabed, the hover on the organ, the camera’s framing and the anglerfish crossing the background.

I built the seabed from free low-poly models instead of modelling anything. A night left no time to model, and low poly holds together under fog and blur where models of mixed detail would not. Each GLB became a small React component, placed by hand around the organ. The eleven models the scene loads weigh 716 KB in all.

The organ on show is one of three, and all three are mounted from the start, at the same spot. Choosing one scales it to full size with GSAP over a second, on power4.out, while the others shrink to zero. Loading each organ on demand would have meant a wait at every switch, and a second loader we had no time to design. On hover, drei’s screen-space Outlines draws a white edge around the organ to show it can be clicked, and a click opens its two panels.

The camera eases toward a position read from the pointer and keeps looking at the organ, so the scene answers the hand without orbit controls a visitor would have to learn.

Retro mode was Colin’s, and it won the prize of the “On veut du gros pixel” challenge. The normal chain runs a water distortion and a tilt-shift blur after tone mapping; the arcade button swaps both for a single Pixelation pass, at a granularity of 15. The scene, its lights and its interactions stay as they are, so the mode cost one flag in the store and one condition in the post-processing component.

We cut what did not fit. The lungs became the brain, a kelp model went, and the kidneys arrived five minutes before the handover.

Engineering notes

Light on a seabed shimmers, and the usual way to draw that is a caustics shader. Colin found a cheaper way: three.js lets a spotlight project a texture, so a looping video of caustics becomes the light’s map, through drei’s useVideoTexture, with decay at 0 so it reaches the sand at full strength from ten units up. I moved it into its own component when we split the scene. It cost no shader work, one light and a video decode per frame. It pays in weight instead: at 2.5 MB, the video is the heaviest file of the site, ahead of the sand’s normal map (1.2 MB) and the ambient sound (1.1 MB).

Looking at the code again, two motions are written per frame rather than per second. The anglerfish moves 0.03 units along its path on every frame, and the camera covers 2.5% of the distance to its target on every frame. On a 60 Hz screen they look as we tuned them; on a 120 Hz screen the fish swims twice as fast and the camera follows the pointer more tightly. Both fixes are a line each: multiply the step by the frame’s delta, and turn the camera’s factor into 1 - Math.exp(-k * delta), which gives the same easing at any frame rate. The fish’s sideways sway already used delta, so on a fast screen it no longer matches its own forward speed.

Outcome

We handed the project in at 7:40 on 6 December, deployed on Netlify with its source public, as the national challenge asked. It won the prize of the “On veut du gros pixel” challenge for its retro mode. The scene is still online as it was handed in: the loader, the ambient sound, the three organs with their panels, the retro mode and a credits page.
