My first GLSL shaders: a 3D carousel that bends and splits around the cursor

I did not expect to be glad to see sines and cosines again. Writing my first shaders brought back the trigonometry I learned in high school without knowing what it was for: the wave that bends each image is a sine, and the ring that holds the images is a sine and a cosine.

The project is a 3D carousel. Twelve stills from Studio Ghibli’s When Marnie Was There hang in a ring that turns as you scroll, and each image ripples, with its red and blue pulled apart around the cursor. It was my first solo project after the shader lessons of Three.js Journey, built the way I learn best: read about something, then make something small that uses it. I am not a shader expert, and this is the tour I would have liked before starting, with the bug that sent me back to the code the same afternoon.

The setup

The scene runs on my own Three.js boilerplate: an Experience singleton owns the renderer, the camera, the resources and the render loop, and each part of the world, the carousel or an image, fetches it with Experience.getInstance(). The shaders are .glsl files imported straight into TypeScript through vite-plugin-glsl, so they get syntax highlighting instead of living in template strings.

The ring itself is plain trigonometry: image i of 12 sits at the angle i / 12 × 2π, at x = sin(angle) × 4.5 and z = cos(angle) × 4.5, and lookAt(0, 0, 0) turns it towards the centre, inside the ring, where the camera stands. Scrolling adds to a rotation speed that loses 30 % of itself every frame on a desktop, and when it falls under 0.0005 rad a frame, the ring keeps drifting at that pace on its own.

A wave in the vertex shader

The vertex shader runs once per vertex and decides where it lands on screen. Each image is a PlaneGeometry with 32 by 32 segments, so 1,089 vertices, enough for a smooth bend. The whole deformation is three lines:

float elevation = sin(modelPosition.x * uFrequency.x) * 0.1;
elevation += sin(modelPosition.y * uFrequency.y) * 0.1;
modelPosition.z += elevation;

A sine of the vertex’s position, between -1 and 1, scaled down to 0.1. uFrequency is a uniform, a value sent from JavaScript and shared by every vertex of the mesh, and it sets how tight the wave is: 3.5 along x and 0 along y, so the images ripple horizontally only.

One detail I only understood later: modelPosition is the vertex in world space, after the mesh’s own position and rotation. The wave stays where it is in the scene, and the images move through it as the ring turns, which is why the bend seems to travel across each one. The shader also declares a uTime that every image updates on each frame and the shader never reads.

Three samples for one pixel

The fragment shader runs once per pixel the image covers and decides its colour. texture2D(uTexture, vUv) reads the image at the UV coordinates handed down by the vertex shader and returns red, green, blue and alpha. While trying it, I realised nothing forces the three colours to come from the same place:

float r = texture2D(uTexture, vUv + uvOffset).r;
float g = texture2D(uTexture, vUv).g;
float b = texture2D(uTexture, vUv - uvOffset).b;

Red is read slightly ahead, blue slightly behind, green stays put, and the edges in the image split into coloured fringes, the chromatic aberration of a cheap lens. It was not in the plan: it came out of poking at the fragment shader, and it costs four texture reads instead of one. A vignette closes the shader: dot(uv, uv) is the squared distance from the centre of the image, and the colour darkens with it.

A lens that follows the cursor

The next step was to make it answer the mouse rather than sit still. The shader compares each pixel with the cursor and keeps the effect within a radius:

vec2 screenUv = gl_FragCoord.xy / uResolution;

float aspect = uResolution.x / uResolution.y;
vec2 diff = screenUv - uMouse;
diff.x *= aspect;

float dist = length(diff);
float mask = smoothstep(uStereoRadius, 0.0, dist);
vec2 dir = dist > 0.001 ? normalize(diff) : vec2(0.0);

vec2 uvOffset = dir * uStereoStrength * mask;
uvOffset.x /= aspect;

gl_FragCoord is the pixel’s position in the canvas; divided by the canvas size, it runs from 0 to 1 on both axes, like the cursor. Its origin is the bottom left corner while the DOM counts from the top, so the cursor’s y is flipped in JavaScript: 1.0 - e.clientY / innerHeight. The difference is multiplied by the aspect ratio before it is measured, otherwise a radius would be wider than tall on a landscape screen, and the offset is divided by it again to come back to 0 to 1 units. smoothstep turns the distance into a mask, 1 under the cursor and 0 beyond uStereoRadius, 0.2 of the screen’s height, and the colours split away from the cursor by up to uStereoStrength, 0.04.

Halfway to the cursor on a Retina screen

On my Retina screen, the lens did not follow the cursor. It sat between the cursor and the bottom left corner, exactly halfway, and at half the size I had set.

gl_FragCoord counts device pixels. With a pixel ratio of 2 the canvas is drawn at twice its CSS size, and I had sent uResolution in CSS pixels. screenUv ran from 0 to 2 instead of 0 to 1, so it matched the cursor at half the cursor’s coordinates, and every distance came out twice too long, which halved the radius. With a ratio of 1 the two units are the same and the bug cannot show. The fix went in two and a half hours after the effect:

image.uniforms.uResolution.value.set(
  sizes.width * sizes.pixelRatio,
  sizes.height * sizes.pixelRatio,
);

The ratio has to be the one the renderer draws at, not window.devicePixelRatio. The project caps it with Math.min(window.devicePixelRatio, 2) and passes the same value to renderer.setPixelRatio. On a screen with a ratio of 3, the raw value would send the lens past the cursor this time, one and a half times as far from the corner.

What is still off

The offset is measured on the screen but applied to the image’s UVs, and the two do not have the same proportions. U runs across the width of the image and v across its height, so a split that should be round comes out stretched by the image’s ratio divided by the window’s. The stills are 1920 by 1038, a ratio of 1.85, so in a 16:10 window the split is about 16 % wider than it is tall, and its size follows how large the image is on screen. The ratios are close enough that it hardly shows. To fix it, I would convert the offset with dFdx(vUv) and dFdy(vUv), which tell how much the UVs change from one pixel to the next on screen. I have not tried it yet.

The demo runs in the browser, and the source is on GitHub.
